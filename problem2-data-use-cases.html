Problem 2 · Data use cases4

Use case 01 of 10 · Label quality audit · The problem

One labeler can quietly teach the AI that a dangerous road is safe

Mission scenario
What it is
Checking that the people who label training images, the annotators, label correctly and consistently before the AI learns from them. In the data they are ANN-01 to ANN-06: Annotator 1 to Annotator 6.
Why it's a problem
The AI copies whatever the labels say. One labeler's mistakes become the model's mistakes, and normal testing can't reveal it, because the test images were labeled by the same people.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Day 2: 4,000 new flood images go to the labeling contractor. A new labeler, Annotator 6, joins to keep up with the volume.
2
The guidelines define a washout as “road surface missing”. They say nothing about undercut shoulders, where the asphalt is still there.
3
Annotator 6 labels most undercut shoulders as healthy road. The retrained model learns that undercut edges are normal, and passes its tests.
4
Day 6: a hospital-route segment goes out as passable. The shoulder collapses under a supply truck; two soldiers are hurt and medical supplies arrive 18 hours late.
What this use case solves: bad labels are caught before they train the model, and the guideline gap is closed on day 2 instead of after an incident.

Problem 2 · Data use cases6

Use case 02 of 10 · Class imbalance · The problem

A model can be 97% accurate and still miss a third of the deadliest hazard

Mission scenario
What it is
A class is one type of thing the AI recognizes: healthy road, debris, undercut shoulder. Class imbalance means the model has seen thousands of examples of some classes and only a few hundred of others.
Why it's a problem
A model can look excellent overall and still fail on the rare class. Only 7% of what the drones see each night is a hazard, so a model that always said “passable” would already be 93% accurate. The rare classes are usually the dangerous ones.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Training data holds 12,000 healthy road images but only 300 undercut shoulders and 250 soft landing zones: both are new this flood season.
2
The model reports 96.7% overall accuracy and is approved.
3
Nobody checks the classes one by one. It catches only 65% of undercut shoulders and 72% of soft landing zones.
4
Commanders trust the headline number, and about one undercut edge in three goes out as passable.
What this use case solves: a good average can no longer hide failure on the classes that decide whether a convoy is safe.

Problem 2 · Data use cases8

Use case 03 of 10 · Accuracy by segment · The problem

An overall score hides where the AI fails: dawn flights in the valleys

Mission scenario
What it is
Splitting results by conditions (time of day, terrain, camera, drone team) instead of trusting one overall number. Each slice is a segment, such as “dawn flights in valleys”.
Why it's a problem
Averages hide weak spots. A model can be reliable overall and far worse in one condition, and that condition may be where the mission matters most.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Valley landing zones can only be flown at dawn, before fog builds.
2
In long shadows, the model struggles to tell soft waterlogged ground from firm ground.
3
Elsewhere it catches 91% of soft landing zones; on dawn valley flights only 60%.
4
A medevac helicopter is sent to a “usable” field, finds soft ground and diverts: 40 minutes lost with a critical patient on board.
What this use case solves: you know where the AI can and can't be trusted, and you fix those spots without retraining everything.

Problem 2 · Data use cases10

Use case 04 of 10 · Threshold optimization · The problem

One default cut-off for every hazard lets uncertain dangers through

Mission scenario
What it is
The AI gives every finding a confidence score from 0 to 1 (“I'm 0.35 sure this shoulder is undercut”). The threshold is the cut-off above which a finding becomes an alert. Optimizing it means setting the cut-off from the real cost of each kind of mistake.
Why it's a problem
Set it too high and real damage scored 0.35 is ignored. Set it too low and every shadow closes a road and sends convoys on 3-hour detours. The right line is different for each hazard.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
The system ships with one default threshold, 0.50, for every hazard.
2
Undercut shoulders are new, so the model is unsure: many score between 0.20 and 0.50.
3
All of those go out as passable: 129 undercut shoulders missed over the mission.
4
Meanwhile minor debris uses the same 0.50 and keeps closing roads crews could simply drive around.
What this use case solves: where to draw the line becomes a costed, per-hazard decision that commanders approve openly.

Use case 05 of 10 · False-alarm root cause · The problem

Constant false alarms teach planners to ignore the real ones

Mission scenario
What it is
A false positive is the AI flagging a hazard that isn't there. Root-cause analysis records why each one happened, so the cause can be removed instead of dismissed again tomorrow.
Why it's a problem
Each false alarm sends a scarce engineer recon team to a good road, or closes it. Worse, it erodes trust: once planners override closures as “always wrong”, a real one gets overridden too.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
At dawn, fresh asphalt patches, wet sheen and tree shadows look like damage.
2
The AI raises about 18 false alarms a night, and only four recon teams are available.
3
By day 4, planners start overriding closures without checking.
4
Day 5: one of the overridden closures is a real landslide. A convoy turns back halfway up the mountain.
What this use case solves: fewer false alarms, so people keep trusting the alarms that are real.

Problem 2 · Data use cases14

Use case 06 of 10 · Drift monitoring · The problem

A new camera can quietly break an approved model mid-mission

Mission scenario
What it is
Drift is when a model that worked at approval quietly gets worse because the incoming data changes: new cameras, new terrain, a new season. Monitoring measures performance every day and raises an alert when it slips.
Why it's a problem
Nothing visibly breaks. The maps still look plausible while accuracy falls, and nobody notices until something goes wrong.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Day 8: Team B joins with a different camera to cover more ground.
2
Its images have different color and contrast, and the model raises many more false alarms on them.
3
Precision (the share of flags that are real) falls to 54% on Team B flights vs 80% for Team A.
4
Planners assume the terrain got worse and reroute around good roads; deliveries slow for a week before anyone connects it to the new camera.
What this use case solves: problems are caught in days, not weeks, and traced to their cause.

Problem 2 · Data use cases16

Use case 07 of 10 · Landing-check gate · The problem

“No damage found” on a blurred image is not the same as safe

Mission scenario
What it is
An automatic check of image quality (motion blur, haze, resolution, coverage) the moment the drone lands, before the crew leaves the site.
Why it's a problem
The AI can't find damage it can't see, but it may still report “no damage found”. On a bad image, that isn't evidence the road is safe, yet it reaches the map as passable.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Wind over a ridge and dawn fog in a valley degrade a sortie's images.
2
The model finds nothing in the blur and marks those segments passable.
3
The crew has already driven two hours to the next site, so going back costs half a night.
4
A convoy meets a washout on one of those segments and has to reverse 3 km on a mountain road at night.
What this use case solves: bad data never produces a “safe” verdict, and crews only recapture when it changes the answer.

Problem 2 · Data use cases18

Use case 08 of 10 · Change detection · The problem

Re-reporting yesterday's hazards buries the one that appeared overnight

Mission scenario
What it is
Comparing tonight's confirmed findings with last night's for the same road segment, and tagging each one new, worse, unchanged or cleared.
Why it's a problem
The same roads are surveyed every night. Without the comparison, every known blockage is reported again as if it were new, and the change that matters gets lost in the list.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Each morning's map lists about 60 hazards, most of them the same as yesterday.
2
The duty officer has 20 minutes to brief commanders and can't see what changed.
3
A new washout on the main supply route overnight is buried in the list.
4
Meanwhile debris that engineers cleared yesterday still shows as closed, so a usable route sits idle.
What this use case solves: commanders see what changed overnight in two minutes, and cleared routes reopen the same morning.

Problem 2 · Data use cases20

Use case 09 of 10 · Review prioritization · The problem

Checking flags in arrival order leaves the riskiest roads unchecked at 06:00

Mission scenario
What it is
Deciding which AI flags the analyst checks first when there isn't time to check them all: by risk, not by the order they arrived.
Why it's a problem
The night-shift analyst can verify about 35 flags before the 06:00 map, and the AI raises about 67 a night. Working first in, first out means the riskiest flags may never get a human check.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
Night sorties over open plains land first; dawn sorties over the valleys, where the main supply and hospital routes run, land last.
2
The analyst works through minor debris on back roads first.
3
At 06:00 half the queue is unchecked, including critical hazards on the hospital route.
4
They go out on the AI's word alone.
What this use case solves: the riskiest calls always get a human check before the map goes out.

Use case 10 of 10 · TRACE lineage · The problem

If you can't explain one call, commanders stop trusting all of them

Mission scenario
What it is
Keeping a complete record behind every AI finding: source image, LiDAR scan, flight conditions, model version, threshold, training batch and reviewer. This is the lineage that TRACE requires.
Why it's a problem
After an incident the first question is “why did the AI say that?”. Without a record there is no answer, and the only safe response is to stop trusting the AI everywhere.
How it goes wrong in the mission
1
After the truck rollover, the review board asks who marked the road passable, on what evidence and with which model.
2
The company can show the map, but not how the call was made.
3
Only 2% of findings released on days 1–5 carry a full record; the training batch is almost never logged.
4
The battalion suspends the AI maps; roads are checked by hand, three times slower.
What this use case solves: every call can be explained and defended, and when something fails the fix is targeted instead of a shutdown.

TRACE check
  • Transparent: image, LiDAR and sortie shown
  • Repeatable: model, threshold and training batch versioned

  • Enforced: released only with all fields
